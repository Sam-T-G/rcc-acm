Putting it together

Batch the crowd, tune for the person

  1. Group people with similar tasteCall each group a cohort
  2. Build one batch per cohort, ahead of timeCache it so it's ready in a blink
  3. Re-rank the batch for each personCheap, and it uses live watch time
  4. Refresh the batch every few minutesNot on every swipe

This is our design, not a claim about how any one app does it. The real idea underneath is common: gather a big pile of candidates cheaply, then rank a short list carefully. First, meet the board.

Meet the board

A tiny computer that runs one program

Send it code over USB and it runs that code, over and over, until you unplug it.

Our kits use the Elegoo UNO R3, which copies this exact open design, so everything on this photo is on your board. The long chip is the ATmega328P: 16 MHz, 32 KB for your program. It does one thing at a time, and does it forever.

We also have three Arduino UNO R4 WiFi boards; the same code runs on those.

Let's name the parts we'll actually touch today.

Meet the board

What we'll use on it

  1. USB: power and code go inThe square port on the left
  2. Pins 0 to 13 turn on or offOn is 5 volts, off is 0
  3. A ~ pin can fake in-betweenThat's PWM, our fan speed control
  4. 5V and GND power your circuitEvery circuit ends at GND

Point at a real board while you talk. The little L light by pin 13 is wired to pin 13 on the board, so it blinks along with our strobe. Handy check that your code uploaded.

The tilde pins on the UNO are 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, and 11. We use 5 for fan speed.

So how does code actually run on this thing?

How code runs

Every sketch has two parts

  1. setup() runs onceAt power on, or when you hit reset
  2. loop() runs foreverTop to bottom, then again
  3. pinMode() says in or outOUTPUT means the pin drives something
  4. delay() waitsAnd the board does nothing else meanwhile

This is the callback: the feed was open once, loop forever. Same shape.

A program for an Arduino is called a sketch. Milliseconds: delay(1000) is one second.

Plant this for later: delay() freezes everything. We'll see why that's fine for a fan.

You write and send sketches with the Arduino IDE.

Everything plugs into the breadboard, so let's see how it's wired inside.

Breadboards

How a breadboard connects

  1. Each short strip of five is one wireTwo legs in one strip are touching
  2. The middle gap splits the stripsChips sit across it
  3. The long rails run the whole length+ for power, - for ground
  4. Nothing is solderedPull it out and try again

The classic mistake: putting both legs of a part in the same strip, which shorts it out. Each leg goes in its own strip.

Chips straddle the gap so each pin gets its own strip.

Wire the light first

Unplug the USB while wiring. Any GND pin on the UNO works.

An LED only lets current through one way, so backwards means dark, not broken. Flip it and try again.

Why the resistor? Here's the math.

Why the resistor

No resistor

The LED pulls as much current as the pin can push. It gets hot, dims, and can wear out the pin.

220 Ω

(5 V − 2 V) ÷ 220 Ω ≈ 14 mA. Bright, and under the 20 mA a pin is happy giving.

Ohm's law: current = voltage ÷ resistance. The pin gives 5 volts; a red LED uses about 2 of them; the resistor gets the other 3. 3 ÷ 220 is about 0.014 amps, 14 milliamps.

Arduino's docs: 20 mA per pin is the comfortable number, 40 mA the absolute limit. That 20 comes back in five slides.

Now the code that makes it strobe.

The strobe sketch

const int LED = 13;

void setup() { pinMode(LED, OUTPUT); }

void loop() {
  digitalWrite(LED, HIGH);   // flash on
  delay(30);
  digitalWrite(LED, LOW);    // dark
  delay(270);
}

Walk it line by line. const int LED = 13 gives pin 13 a name. setup() makes it an output once. loop() turns it on, waits 30 milliseconds, turns it off, waits 270, forever.

Upload it. The LED and the board's L light flash together about three times a second.

Full sketch, compiled for the UNO and the UNO R4 WiFi on 2026-10-05.

Let's see what those two numbers are really doing.

Build · step one

What makes it a strobe

  1. On for 30 msA short, sharp flash
  2. Off for 270 msMostly dark
  3. One cycle is 300 msAbout three flashes a second
  4. Shrink the gapFast enough, your eyes see a steady light

The picture is slowed down ten times so it's easy to follow.

A strobe is a short flash with a long gap. Equal on and off is a blink. Try delay(10) and delay(20): above roughly 50 flashes a second most people stop seeing the flicker. That's also how dimming works, and it's the idea behind fan speed in a minute.

Light done. Now the fan, and the fan can't plug straight into a pin.

Build · step two

A pin can't push a motor

  1. A pin is happy at 20 mA40 mA is its limit
  2. A motor needs way moreMost of all when it starts
  3. It kicks back voltage spikesWhen it switches off
  4. So a chip does the heavy liftingThe L293D, with its own power

Elegoo's own lesson says it too: don't drive the motor straight from the board's pins; use a driver chip and a separate supply.

The L293D can hand out up to 600 mA per channel (TI datasheet), and the D means it has diodes built in that soak up the kickback.

Analogy: the pin is a light switch, the chip is the breaker panel.

So let's get to know the chip.

Build · step two

Meet the L293D

  1. Notch up, pin 1 is top leftCount down the left, up the right
  2. Pins 16 and 8 to the + rail16 runs the chip, 8 runs the motor
  3. The middle four go to groundPins 4, 5, 12, and 13
  4. Motor on 3 and 6, Arduino on 1, 2, 7Pin 1 is speed, 2 and 7 are direction

Pin numbers and roles from the TI L293D datasheet: 1 is enable for channel 1 and 2; 2 and 7 are inputs; 3 and 6 are outputs; 4, 5, 12, 13 are ground; 8 is motor supply; 16 is logic supply. Pins 9 to 15 drive a second motor; we leave them empty.

Straddle the gap with the notch pointing toward the end of the breadboard so the numbering is easy.

The chip needs its own power, so let's set that up.

Build · step two

Give the motor its own power

  1. Clip the power module onto one endIts pins drop into both rails
  2. Set both jumpers to 5VNot 3.3V
  3. Plug in the 9V adapter, press the switchIts little light comes on
  4. Wire the UNO's GND to the - railOne shared ground or nothing works

The module turns 9 volts into a steady 5 for the rails. Elegoo warns not to feed it more than 9V. The UNO stays on USB; only its GND joins the rail. Do not connect the UNO's 5V pin to the rail.

Shared ground is the most common reason the fan sits still: the chip can't read the Arduino's signals without a common reference.

Rails are live. Now the actual fan wiring.

Build · step two

Wire the fan

  1. UNO pin 5 to chip pin 1Speed
  2. UNO 3 and 4 to chip 2 and 7Direction
  3. Chip 3 and 6 to the motorEither way round is fine
  4. Press the blade onto the shaftFingers clear when it runs

Unplug USB and switch the module off while wiring. Swapping the motor wires only changes which way it spins.

These pin choices (5, 3, 4) match the code on the next slide. If you use different pins, change the code to match.

Here's the code that turns it on.

The fan sketch

const int EN = 5, IN1 = 3, IN2 = 4;   // to chip pins 1, 2, 7
void setup() {
  pinMode(EN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(IN1, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(IN2, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(IN1, HIGH);   // one way
  digitalWrite(IN2, LOW);
  analogWrite(EN, 180);      // speed, 0 to 255
}
void loop() { }              // nothing to do

IN1 HIGH and IN2 LOW means spin one way; swap them and it spins the other way; both LOW stops it.

analogWrite(EN, 180) sets the speed. loop() is empty, and the fan keeps spinning anyway, because PWM runs in the chip's hardware on its own.

If it twitches but doesn't start, try 255, or give the blade a flick.

So what does 180 out of 255 actually mean?

Build · step two

How the speed number works

  1. 255: always onFull speed
  2. 128: on half the timeSwitching about a thousand times a second
  3. 50: on a fifth of the timeSlow, and it might stall
  4. The motor averages it outIt can't start and stop that fast

PWM, pulse width modulation: the pin is only ever fully on or fully off, but it switches so fast the motor feels the average. On the UNO, pin 5 switches at about 980 times a second (Arduino analogWrite docs).

Same trick as the strobe going steady when the gap gets short.

Two sketches. Now make them one.

Build · step three

Put them in one sketch

  1. Start from the strobe sketch
  2. Add the fan's three pin names on topEN, IN1, IN2
  3. Add the fan's setup lines to setup()Next to pinMode(LED, OUTPUT)
  4. Leave loop() as the strobePWM keeps the fan going

This is the payoff from the delay() warning: delay() freezes loop(), but the fan doesn't care, because the hardware keeps its PWM running while loop() blinks.

Here's the finished setup().

The merged setup()

void setup() {
  pinMode(EN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(IN1, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(IN2, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(LED, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(IN1, HIGH);
  digitalWrite(IN2, LOW);
  analogWrite(EN, 180);      // fan on, and it stays on
}

With the four pin names at the top (EN, IN1, IN2, LED) and the strobe loop() below, that's the whole sketch. It compiles for the UNO and UNO R4 WiFi (checked 2026-10-05).

Make the fan look frozen

  1. Flash much fasterShort flashes, short gaps
  2. Tune it to the bladesOne flash per blade, same spot
  3. The blades look frozenYou only see them in one place
  4. Now you've built a tachometerIt measures the fan's speed

This is how a stroboscope works. In a bright room it's subtle, so shade the fan with a hand or a notebook.
