Names, in plain words. Breadboard: the white board with holes where circuits get built. Power module: the small board that clips onto one end and turns 9 volts into 5 volts for the rails. 9V battery: powers the module through its barrel plug (the 9V wall adapter does the same job if a battery is dead). UNO R3 board: the Arduino, the little computer. USB cable: power and code from the laptop. Jumper wires: male to male, a pin on both ends.

Red LED: a light that only works one way around (long leg is +). 220 ohm resistor: slows the electricity so the LED doesn't burn out; the resistors are grouped by value with a 220 label [CHECK on a real kit]; if a strip has no label, 220 ohm is red, red, brown, gold on a 4-band resistor or red, red, black, black, brown on a 5-band one. Button: four legs, a gap you close by pressing. Fan blade and motor: the blade pushes onto the motor shaft. Got everything? Here's the one idea behind every circuit.

Circuits from zero

Every circuit is a loop

  1. Electricity leaves +, comes back to -No way back, nothing flows
  2. Your part sits in the loopIt lights up, spins, or beeps
  3. Break the loop anywhere, it all stopsThat's all a switch does
  4. Something in the loop has to slow itWith nothing there, it's a short

Fills in on its own. One press lands the rest; the next press moves on.

No code yet. Code only turns things on and off; the circuit does the work, so it comes first.

Think of water in a closed pipe. The power supply is the pump, the wires are pipes, your part is a water wheel. If the water can't get back to the pump, nothing moves.

The minus side is called ground. Every loop today ends at ground.

A short is + wired straight to - with nothing in between. Nothing slows it down, so wires get hot and the power module shuts off or breaks.

You'll build loops on a breadboard. Here's how it's wired inside.

Circuits from zero

How a breadboard connects

  1. Each row of five holes is connectedTwo legs in one row touch
  2. The middle gap splits the rowsButtons sit across it
  3. The long rails run the whole length+ for power, - for ground
  4. Every hole has a name, like a10Letter down the side, number along the top

Fills in on its own. One press lands the rest; the next press moves on.

The most common mistake: both legs of a part in the same row. Then electricity goes around the part instead of through it. Each leg gets its own row.

Hole names: the letters a to j are printed down the side, the numbers along the top. a10 is the hole in line a, at number 10. All five holes a10 to e10 are connected.

Nothing is soldered, so a mistake just means pulling a part out and trying again.

delay counts in milliseconds, so 1000 is one second and 200 is a fifth of a second.

Arduino calls a program a sketch.

So who calls setup() and loop()? Arduino does, from a main() you never see.

What Arduino runs for you

// Arduino's main.cpp, simplified
int main() {
  init();          // set up the board's timers
  setup();         // your setup(), once
  while (true) {
    loop();        // your loop(), forever
  }
}

A sketch is C++. Every C++ program starts at main(), and Arduino writes main() for you. It calls setup() once, then calls loop() inside a loop that never ends. So loop() is just a function that gets called over and over.

Simplified from Arduino's real main.cpp (checked 2026-10-07). The real one uses for (;;), which does the same thing as while (true), plus some USB and serial housekeeping.

You can write your own main() in a sketch, but then you have to call init() yourself, or delay() stops working. setup() and loop() are the normal way, and every Arduino example online uses them.

One more thing about that brown wire.

Check in

Ground is the floor

  1. Volts are a differenceLike height, measured from the floor
  2. Ground is that floor, 0 VEverything is measured from it
  3. The pin says 5 V, the servo reads itBut 5 V above what?
  4. So every part shares one groundThe brown wire does that

5 volts only means something compared to something else. "The shelf is 5 feet high." High from where?
